Supplementary Appendix 1. Data analysis overview, analytic notes for individual cohorts,
and further details on covariate and outcome definitions and ascertainment
1.1 Overview:
In order to determine the cohorts eligible for this paper, in February/March 2023 all United States-based cohorts in
CKD-PC with cardiovascular outcome data available were contacted regarding their interest in this topic and to
confirm availability of data. 48 cohorts were contacted, one was not able to participate due to a lack of all-cause
mortality data and one was not able to send de-identified data in time for analyses, resulting in 46 cohorts in this
analysis. Cohorts were required to have at least 5 years of follow-up for the 95th percentile of time to events [cases].
As previously described,51 the collaborating cohorts were asked to compile a dataset with approximately 25
variables (Required: main exposure [age, sex, race/ethnicity, history of CVD, smoking, diabetes, systolic blood
pressure, antihypertensive medications, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, statin use, BMI, eGFR], outcome [fatal
and non-fatal myocardial infarction, fatal and non-fatal ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, fatal and non-fatal heart
failure hospitalization, all-cause mortality]; In addition data were captured if available on: social deprivation index,
albuminuria, and hemoglobin A1c). To be consistent across cohorts, the CKD-PC Data Coordinating Center sent
definitions for those variables to participating cohorts. We instructed studies not to impute any variables.

The CKD-PC data request and processing procedures are as follows. After obtaining opt-in preferences from cohorts
for the topics for each phase, the Data Coordinating Center (DCC) requests de-identified data using a specific data
request document describing the variables and preferred definitions needed for the current phase of the CKD-PC.
Cohorts work with the DCC on any data use agreements, IRB approvals, and other logistic issues for de-identified
data transfer. The DCC also advises on any differences in definitions or questions on data formatting. Cohorts then
provide de-identified data (in whatever program format, e.g., Stata, SAS, csv) via a secure data transfer provided or
their own secure transfer program/platform. Data is stored on a secure password protected network server that is
only accessed by limited faculty and staff (<10). All those faculty and staff have completed HIPAA and CITI
certification and have signed internal data use agreements to not use the data for any other than stated purposed and
to not remove the data from that network drive. The CKD-PC does not share data with any external parties. Once
data is received and stored in the network drive, the DCC programmer reviews the data and the data dictionary
provided by the cohort to check for any missing information, outliers, and potentials issues with variable units,
dates, etc. Any questions are sent to the cohort representatives for data checking and cleaning. Further data checking
is done throughout the analysis process for each CKD-PC paper, including a review from a cohort representative of
all tables and figures to confirm their cohort representation.

For the 46 cohorts in this specific study, the DCC at Johns Hopkins University conducted the analysis. Then the
DCC meta-analyzed the estimates across cohorts using Stata.

As detailed in our previous reports,38 each cohort was instructed to standardize their serum creatinine and report its
method when available. The reported creatinine standardization allows grouping studies into studies that reported
using a standard IDMS traceable method or conducted some serum creatinine standardization to IDMS traceable
methods (ARIC, CARDIA, CRIC, Geisinger, JHS, Rancho Bernardo, REGARDS) and studies where the creatinine
standardization was not done (CHS, Framingham, MESA). For those cohorts without standardization, the creatinine
levels were reduced by 5%, the calibration factor used to adjust non-standardized MDRD Study samples to IDMS.37,

1.2 Details of the analysis:
Survival analysis was conducted stratified by sex on the age scale allowing for left censoring at the age of entry and
right censoring at the age of death or exit from follow-up. For each outcome (CVD, ASCVD, HF, CHD and for use
in competing risks models death prior to each of these) we conducted a separate Cox regression.

Meta analysis of hazard ratios from all development cohorts was conducted using univariate random effects models.
The meta-analyzed hazard ratio was applied to all development models in a Cox model to obtain the adjusted
baseline cumulative hazard (centered at age 55, non-HDLC 3.5, HDLC 1.3, SBP 130 and absence of other risk
factors). This baseline log cumulative hazard was smoothed using a cubic spline with knots at 35, 55, 65, 75 and 85
of age (the approximate 1, 25, 50, 75 and 99 percentiles). For each outcome, age specific log hazards were
calculated and the median at each age and sex specific group were calculated. The overall result was then smoothed
using a linear age.

Details on the meta-analysis method are as follows: All analyses were performed separately in each cohort. Next,
estimates were pooled using random-effects meta-analysis using the Stata “metan” command (Stata version 16,
College Station, TX). This two-stage approach allows close evaluation of individual study results to ensure no
errors. The random-effects meta-analysis allows for estimation of both within study random variation and between
study systematic variation (heterogeneity; International Journal of Epidemiology 2010;39:1345–1359). Given the
complexity and number of the competing risk models and the size of the datasets, we employ a univariate meta-
analysis rather than a multivariate approach (e.g., “mvmeta”), which requires prohibitively long computing time. In
similar analyses, “metan” and “mvmeta” had <2% difference in estimates. We routinely share statistical code upon
request. The source data were released to us using specific data use agreements so we cannot share the data
themselves but we reply to queries including running analyses to check on the results using the full datasets.

Absolute risk of each CVD outcome was calculated using a competing risks model of the cause specific hazard of
the CVD outcome and its paired mortality (free of this type of CVD). This model used the meta-analyzed hazard
ratios and median baseline hazard.

Discrimination was calculated using time-dependent Harrel’s C-statistic. Calibration was estimated by graphing the
observed risk within each decile of predicted risk. The slope was calculated as a summary measure of calibration
with a slope of 1.0 indicating good calibration (<1 indicating over-prediction and >1 indicating under-prediction).
Deciles were combined if they had <5 events. Calibration was examined overall and within subgroups.
Improvement in calibration between the base model and larger models were tested based on a Wilcoxon matched
pair rank-sum test of the paired comparisons between the absolute value of the difference between the slope and 1.0
in the two models being compared. NRI was calculated using the event rate method which uses the event rates as the
cutoff to calculate sensitivity and specificity for each model and the difference in each is summed to obtain the
NRI.62

Calibration figures applied minimum criteria to focus on reliable data. Specifically, the follow-up period was
shortened from 10 years to 8 or 5 years if the 95th percentile for events was 8-10 years or 5-8 years respectively. In
addition, deciles were merged when there were fewer than 5 events in the decile. Cohorts with fewer than 10 events
for the specific outcome, subgroup and follow-up time were not plotted.

Regression approximations were calculated for a more rapid calculation of the competing risks models. For each
model, the logit (log (risk/1-risk)) absolute risk was fit using linear regression on the same predictors as in the
relative hazard model plus age. Approximations were considered adequate since their r-square was greater than 0.99
and visual inspection of full model vs. approximate model absolute risk calculations indicated very tight agreement
at risks below 10% and excellent agreement at higher risks.



1.3 Notes for individual cohorts:

ARIC: Visit 4 was used as baseline, which was conducted between 1996-98. Hemoglobin A1c was not available in
this study.

CARDIA: Year 10 was used as baseline, which was conducted between 1995-96. Hemoglobin A1c was not
available in this study.

CHS: Visit 5 was used as baseline, which was conducted between 1995-98. This cohort had urine albumin-to-
creatinine ratio measures at a different visit of HDL cholesterol, thus not included in the Base+albuminuria analysis.
Hemoglobin A1c was not available in this study.

CRIC: Visit 1 was used as baseline, which was conducted between 2003-07. Of the ACR data, 25% were estimated
from PCR. Hemoglobin A1c was not available in this study.

Framingham: Visit 1 was used as baseline, which was conducted between 1995-98. Hemoglobin A1c was not
available in this study.

Geisinger: Baseline was defined as first outpatient visit with both serum creatinine and systolic blood pressure
measurements after 1/1/2009, as well as enrolled in the system for at least one year. Albumin to creatinine ratio was
abstracted from the closest lab measurement within 3 years prior to the baseline. When albuminuria data were not
available, the closest proteinuria (protein to creatinine ratio, PCR, or dipstick) within 3 years prior to the baseline
were used to estimate ACR using a validated algorithm.42 Of the ACR data 4.3% were estimated from PCR and 53%
from dipstick.

JHS: Visit 1 was used as baseline, which was conducted between 2000-04. History of HF was not available in this
study. JHS participants that were also in ARIC were excluded.

MESA: Visit 1 was used as baseline, which was conducted between 2000-02. Hemoglobin A1c was not available at
baseline in this study.

OLDW: This study used de-identified electronic health record (EHR) data from the Optum Labs Data Warehouse
(OLDW). The database contains longitudinal health information on enrollees and patients, representing a mixture of
ages and geographical regions across the United States. The EHR-derived data includes a subset of EHR data that
has been normalized and standardized into a single database.29 Cohort inclusion criteria was more than 50 events of
ASCVD after excluding missing values of main exposure variables and more than 5-year of 95th percentile of
follow-up in events. One OLDW cohort with >70% missing BMI data was excluded. Baseline was considered first
serum creatinine after 2008 at a visit with a blood pressure measurement and in the health system for at least a year.
Smoking status might be under measured in this study. We validated separately in each cohort in this study. No
cause of death information available. ASCVD was defined by ICD codes from encounters.

Social deprivation index (SDI) during 2015-2020 was linked to the zip code data at the individual participant level
by OLDW staff. SDI was rounded to the nearest decile from 1 to 10. The year used for assigning the zip code for
SDI was the closest year to the baseline for each participant and excluded if >5 years before or >3 years after the
patient’s baseline year. SDI missing value indicator identified patients whose zip code of residence was missing.

Albumin to creatinine ratio was abstracted from the closest lab measurement within 3 years prior to the baseline.
When albuminuria data were not available, the closest proteinuria (protein to creatinine ratio, PCR, or dipstick)
within 3 years prior to the baseline were used to estimate ACR using a validated algorithm.42 Of the ACR data
0.81% were estimated from PCR and 79% from dipstick.

Rancho Bernardo: Visit 1 was used as baseline, which was conducted between 1992-97. Hemoglobin A1c was not
available in this study.

REGARDS: The initial in-home visit was used as baseline, which was conducted between 2003-07. History of
suspected HF was not used in the analyses.

1.4 Numbers of missing covariates, N (%)
Our consortium includes both research cohorts and clinical databases. The main mechanisms of missingness seems
somewhat different in these two cohort types. Specifically, in the former, a set of variables are evaluated based on a
prespecified protocol, and the missingness is often due to lack of specimens, difficulty of measurement in specific
participants, or lack of consent for specific tests. Thus, typically, individuals with missing data are less healthy than
those without missing data. In the latter, data are collected according to clinical routine or indication. Thus, missing
data may be seen in healthier populations. Nonetheless, we first extracted individuals with available data of
variables required for analyses (i.e., age, sex, race/ethnicity, history of CVD, smoking, diabetes, systolic blood
pressure, antihypertensive medications, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, statin use and body mass index) as well
as eGFR given overall wide availability in our datasets. The display below summarizes the missing rate of race,
albuminuria (either by urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, or urine dipstick), and
hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) used in this study by each cohort.
